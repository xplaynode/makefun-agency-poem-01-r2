# Candidates — agency-poem-01 round 2b

All candidates below were invented in this session. Most use the proven
winning shape: a longer narrative scene about delegated or shared agency —
acting in someone else's name — with specific details and a turn where the
power to act is handed back or its cost lands. A few use other shapes for
diversity. No cage/bird, key-under-mat, or wind/tiller images are used.

## C01 — The Interpreter

In the courtroom I turned his Javanese into the judge's English,
sentence by sentence, including the part where he lied about the fence.
My job was his voice, not his conscience. I said it flat.
Then the judge asked, "Do you understand you may refuse?"
He looked at me, not her. I translated the question twice,
slower the second time, so the choosing would be his, not mine.
He was quiet a long time. "Then I refuse," he said, in my mouth.

## C02 — Power of Attorney, Tuesday

The bank clerk pushed the form across and said, "You sign, you're the son."
For three years I had paid my father's bills from his account,
rent, electricity, the woman who came Tuesdays to clean.
I knew his balance better than my own. I never bought a thing.
That Tuesday the clerk offered a credit card in his name, my signature.
I slid the paper back unsigned. "Ask him," I said. "He's slow, not gone."
We waited twenty minutes for him to be wheeled down from dialysis.

## C03 — Her Voice on the Phone

After the funeral my neighbour asked me to answer her phone.
"Tell them I'm not ready," she said. So I told callers she was resting,
told the insurance man she would call Thursday, told her sister the truth.
On the ninth day a caller asked for her directly, by her first name.
I held the receiver out across the kitchen table.
She took it with both hands, like something hot. "It's me," she said.

## C04 — The Apprentice's Cut

For a year I held the board while Pak Dedi sawed, and fetched, and swept.
He never let me cut the good teak. "Your eye isn't ready," he said.
The morning his hands swelled too stiff to grip the saw,
he set the pencil line himself, then stepped back from the bench.
"Now your eye decides," he said. I cut slow, and the line held.
He ran a thumb down the edge and did not pick up the plane to fix it.

## C05 — Voting by Proxy

Mrs. Lim filled in her ballot at her kitchen table, sealed it,
and wrote my name on the proxy form in careful block letters.
"Straight to the box," she said. "Don't open it, don't fix it."
In the queue I wanted to fix it. She had marked the man I hated.
I posted it sealed, exactly as instructed, and bought her noodles.
That, I think, is the whole lesson: her choice riding in my hand,
and my hand doing nothing clever with it at all.

## C06 — Reading Aloud

My sister's eyes were bandaged for a week after the operation,
so I read her messages aloud — all of them, even the cruel one
from the boyfriend, even the bill she could not pay.
On the last night she said, "Skip nothing tomorrow either."
In the morning the bandages came off. She took the phone from me
mid-sentence, squinting, and finished reading it herself, out loud,
while I sat there with my hands suddenly, happily useless.

## C07 — The Frozen Winner

For nineteen days I signed for my mother in the rehab ward —
admission forms, therapy consents, the pharmacy chit
for pills in a paper cup, two white, one yellow.
I learned to write her name, Ratna Wijaya, in her looping hand,
and hated how good I got at it.
She watched every signature. "The R is wrong," she said once.
"Again." I tore the copy up and signed it slower.
On discharge day the nurse slid the last form to me out of habit.
My mother pulled the clipboard toward herself with her good hand.
"Today I do it," she said. The pen shook. The line wandered.
It took her four minutes to finish what took me four seconds.
The nurse started to help, then stopped, then witnessed it.
That shaky name, out of its box, taking up the whole line —
nobody in that room offered to write over it.

## C08 — Say No for Me

"Say no for me," she said. "You know I'm bad at it."
So I declined the extra shifts, the loan, the cousin's scheme,
each no clean in my voice, borrowed from her wanting.
Then her boss asked if she would lead the new team.
Before I could answer she stepped past me into the doorway.
"That one I say myself," she said. "Yes. I start Monday."

## C09 — The Dictated Letter

He dictated from the hospital bed, word for word, and I typed:
the apology to his brother, thirty years late, no excuses in it.
Twice I wanted to soften a sentence and twice I typed it as said.
At the end he asked me to read it back without fixing anything.
I did. He nodded at the worst sentence most of all.
"Send it as mine," he said, "not as your better version."

## C10 — Hands on the Contract

The lawyer read the settlement aloud because my client reads slowly.
Every clause, she stopped and asked him, "Do you want this?"
He said yes to the money, yes to the apology printed in the paper.
At the clause that banned him ever speaking of it again, he stopped her.
"No," he said. "Cross that one out or I walk."
She crossed it out. His mark at the bottom took up half the page.

## C11 — Short Lyric: Refusal

I can be told the hour, the place, the uniform, the song.
I can be told the price of bread and pay it, mostly.
But the small word in my chest keeps its own hours:
it wakes before I do, it has already decided
which orders it will carry and which it will set down
gently, on the table, like a cup that is not mine to drink.

## C12 — The Agent I Sent

I told my agent: cancel everything in March, I need the month empty.
It cancelled the dentist, the flights, my father's eightieth, efficiently.
I stared at the blank calendar I had ordered and felt robbed by obedience.
So I rewrote the instruction: ask me before anything with a person's name.
Now it pauses. I answer. The month filled back up, slower, by choice —
mine, this time, because I had finally written the rule I actually meant.

## C13 — Second Person: The Consent Form

You hold the pen above the consent form for the operation.
The surgeon has explained it twice. Your daughter offers to explain again.
You wave her off — not from pride, from ownership:
if this goes wrong, it will go wrong on your word, not hers.
You sign in the shaky capitals you have used since school.
The form is ordinary. The handwriting is the whole biography.

## C14 — Grandfather Orders Lunch

For eighty years my grandfather ordered for the whole table.
That Sunday his eyes could not make out the new menu's small print,
so he handed it to me — not the choosing, just the reading.
I read every dish, including the ones I knew he would dismiss.
He dismissed four, chose the fifth, and changed the rice to porridge.
The menu went back and forth between us twice more before he nodded.
Nobody ordered for him. That was the point, and we all kept it.

## C15 — The Stand-In

At the audition I read my friend's monologue because her train was late —
her words, her grief about her father, in my unpractised voice.
Halfway through, the director stopped me. "You're holding back," he said.
"I'm holding her place," I said. "There's a difference."
She arrived breathless as they called her name a second time,
took the script from my hands still warm, and began from the top, louder.

## C16 — Signing for the Neighbour

For two years I signed the school forms for the boy next door,
because his mother's English came out careful and slow at offices
and the school liked things fast. I wrote her name with permission.
At the citizenship ceremony she signed the register herself,
letter by letter, while the officer waited without impatience.
She turned the page toward me afterwards so I could see it:
her name, in her own hand, taking exactly as long as it needed.
